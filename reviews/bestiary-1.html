Trailomon · Creature direction · November 1, 2026

Know the creature. Discover the combination.

My recommendation: give each species its own signature behavior, ability and limitation. Let biology and interactions carry the strategy. A fixed class system may be unnecessary.

Already your direction: at least 300 species; connections to their environments; combat specialists, prey, food animals, protectors, fast creatures and unusual wildlife. They do not all need a travel job. Six species currently exist. The 12-class proposal is withdrawn. S28's playable wagon ambush is still in progress.

Six examples of the world this could create

Working concepts only. No new art pass or claim that these are implemented.

Guttersail

A ravine animal folds a deep membrane around its body, then opens it to ride updrafts. It builds momentum before a dive, then becomes vulnerable while grounded.

Hushgrub

A reed-bed predator hunts through soil vibrations. It ambushes the fastest attacker. A deliberate tremor reveals it; slowing down can also foil its hunt.

Tallowback

A drought-country grazer stores reserves along its back. It spends those reserves to survive a fight. Some are farm animals; a wild herd is formidable.

Rattleloom

A gully dweller stretches fibers between loose pebbles to detect footsteps. Its tether catches a rushing enemy, opening a moment for an ally to strike.

Borrowbill

A small floodplain animal picks parasites from big grazers. In danger it shelters on a companion, surrendering its own attacks to protect their shared perch.

Stillmouth

A motionless pool predator learns repeated movements. It punishes a predictable attacker. It can exist chiefly as a dangerous, fascinating fighter.

The design test: can we explain its body, habitat, food and signature trick in a few sentences? Its ecology can explain its abilities without requiring a terrain bonus in every battle.

B1Abilities replace types

Build the first expanded roster without a universal type chart or fixed class roster. Each species gets a signature active, passive and legible weakness. Learn “this one counters repeated strikes” instead of memorizing an elemental multiplier.

Why: S28 already needs independent attacks, repeating abilities and passives. Individual kits fit that foundation and your preference for original identities.

Cost: more kit and enemy-preview design; unfamiliar abilities must be readable before they punish you. Recommendation: YES. Test with the existing six before locking the whole roster.

B2Combinations come from behavior

Use a small vocabulary of visible events: brace, charge, expose, bind, shed, interrupt. Rattleloom binds a charger; a precision hunter exploits the opening. A defender blocks; Cindercoil follows up. Combinations emerge from what creatures do, without assigning either to a type.

Why: our wagon already makes interception useful. S28's block → follow-up is a first test of the same principle.

Cost: a bounded reaction system, explicit triggers and loop prevention. Extend the six kits in S29 before adding more verbs. Recommendation: YES.

B3Creatures leave the battlefield changed

Try one small terrain effect after the combat foundation: a creature loosens the ground, leaving one position difficult to brace in. Another can settle it. Weather may affect duration. The interaction has an understandable physical cause.

Why: expedition terrain and weather already exist, but the new battle model does not yet use them. This connects habitat to combat more deeply than a hidden +10% bonus.

Cost: one visible field-state experiment, targeting previews and persistence checks; avoid dozens of stacking field conditions. Recommendation: promising, after the six-kit test.

B4Some creatures make a living together

Prototype one symbiotic pair. A small parasite-picker can perch on any sufficiently large companion: it gives up its attack, shares protection and leaves when its host falls. This works because of their bodies and behavior, not a special friendship multiplier.

Why: our three-companion limit makes a partner that changes another creature's behavior a meaningful team choice.

Cost: a new targeting/attachment rule and clear host indicators. No hundreds-of-pairs compatibility table. Recommendation: explore later; keep S28 focused.

B5Journeys teach a local trick

Prototype one optional learned ability choice for an existing species. A Reedskipper raised in flood channels learns to redirect a rush; one that works still pools learns a patient ambush. Choose one learned trick at camp, preserving its species identity.

Why: we already have habits, remembered journeys and different route conditions. A specific learned trick could make returning with an old companion worthwhile.

Cost: replace or extend an existing habit, clear unlock conditions and save compatibility. No mandatory repeated grind. Regional skins do not inflate the 300-species count. Recommendation: a later replayability experiment.

B6A food web, not a loot table

First make diet visible in field notes and one authored encounter: a rain-displaced prey herd attracts a predator near the road. Food animals can also be bought or traded as provisions at settlements. Winning against an arbitrary wild creature does not automatically produce meat.

Why: you want food animals and wildlife with purposes beyond helping the wagon. Existing lore allows wildlife predation; the current game has no hunting system.

Cost: diet data and a small encounter/trade slice. Hunting, slaughter controls and crew-as-food mechanics require their own design; this proposal does not add them. Recommendation: useful world-building, after the combat slice.

B7The roster moves through the world

Prototype a small seasonal or weather-driven migration on the current route. Tracks and local reports tell you where a species has moved. Collecting becomes following a living world: a rainy trip reveals a different opportunity from a dry one.

Why: saved tracks, field observations and seeded weather already exist. The current search/deadline tension means extra discoveries cannot simply demand more paid detours.

Cost: a tiny conditional encounter pool, reliable clues and a pacing comparison. Preserve ways to encounter missed species on later trips. Recommendation: strong replayability candidate once expedition integration is stable.

B8Build the 300-entry index in deliberate batches

Start with 24 concise representative species briefs, including our six. Then plan stable slots and regional coverage for at least 300, author the roster in reviewed batches of 20–30, and implement regional subsets. Each entry records habitat, diet, silhouette, behavior, ability/passive, counterplay and production status.

Why: only six species are implemented. A numbered list of 300 names alone would not establish 300 compelling creatures, encounters or playable kits.

Cost: substantial ongoing content production. First deliverable is the 24-species design sample and index schema; art remains deferred. Reserve slots honestly, count distinct species, and track planned versus playable. Recommendation: YES. The final game target remains 300 minimum.

Reference and integration notes

Pokémon's official species records distinguish identity, category, ability and type. Its regional forms connect change to local adaptation. Those are useful structural references; these proposals are original design work, not claims about Pokémon's mechanics.

Keep the Oregon Trail expedition, increasing remoteness, small traveling crew and Astronarch-style attacks. Broader storage, 300-species field-guide browsing and later regions need roadmap work. B1/B2 establish the combat design language; B8 starts the content pipeline. Other YES choices become bounded follow-up experiments after S28/S29. Your comments can change that priority.
