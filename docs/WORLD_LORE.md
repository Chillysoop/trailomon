# Trailomon world and lore

Creative proposal | October 30, 2026

A traveler leaves a familiar depot with a wagon and a few animals that know things the traveler does not. Beyond the last provision shop, wheel ruts grow faint, maps lose their confident lines, and the country begins to follow the habits of its wildlife. Each successful journey makes a farther journey possible.

This is the proposed setting for Trailomon. The six existing species, six landmarks, orchard delivery, and companion relationships are its foundations. The histories, people, ecological details, and later regions below are new creative proposals, not implemented content. The [game design](GAME_DESIGN.md) remains the authority for the playable scope; the [auto-battle proposal](AUTO_BATTLES.md) develops the combat direction.

## The country beyond the road

The settled valleys are small patches of dependable ground in a much larger, poorly mapped country. People have mills, orchards, ferries, iron tools, printed notices, and glass windows. They can repair a wagon very well. They cannot promise that the road will still be where the map puts it after a wet spring.

Rivers move between channels. Banks collapse into old crossings. Burrowing animals open drainage beneath a meadow. Herds maintain passages through dense scrub, then abandon them when their grazing changes. The best maps combine measured distances with observations: where a certain animal drinks, which slope stays dry, and what a moth does before the wind comes up.

Transport is limited by that country. Barges serve the lower rivers, but falls and seasonal shallows interrupt them. Broad permanent roads are expensive to maintain beyond the settled valleys. A light wagon can carry a household's provisions, stop wherever the crew needs, and follow a route that a heavy freight train could not. Small travelers carry things whose value exceeds their weight: medicine, tools, letters, grafts, and knowledge of the way ahead.

The traveler begins as someone competent enough to take a modest contract. They need no secret ancestry or exceptional gift. Their particular achievement is learning to travel with this particular crew.

### How the frontier advances

The central progression is distance from reliable knowledge. More money or a stronger creature cannot remove the need to interpret a place.

| Reach of the journey | What the player can rely on | What becomes uncertain | Creative direction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Settled approaches | Shops, signposts, known grazing, frequent wheel tracks | Daily weather and ordinary mishaps | Gates, clipped hedges, repaired bridges, voices beyond the screen |
| Last maintained road | One final supply stop, seasonal shelters, travelers' reports | Crossing conditions, detours, how old a report is | Patches in roadwork, leaning posts, closed stalls, growing gaps between roofs |
| Surveyed fringe | A few measured landmarks and old observations | Which passages remain usable and where supplies can be gathered | Sparse cairns, faded marks, abandoned survey camps, animal trails crossing wheel ruts |
| Beyond the surveys | Visible terrain, companion behavior, equipment, judgment | Connections between landmarks and the cost of reaching the next refuge | Unbroken horizons, living landmarks, unfamiliar tracks, long stretches without human sound |

The first playable occupies the first two stages and hints at the third. Later expeditions extend into the latter stages. A farther expedition can be selected directly once available; the player should not have to replay an easy opening journey every time.

Remote country still has things to discover and people with their own knowledge. An occasional fisher, seasonal keeper, or traveling surveyor may know one valley intimately. No single map covers all of it, and no encounter exists merely to congratulate the player for arriving first.

## The first parcel

The orchard cuttings are several local varieties carried together, including a small, tough cooking pear formerly grown at Lantern Reach. Cuttings from the old upland trees were sent down to Fernwake years ago. Their descendants now supply this return shipment.

The destination is a small, seasonal settlement at the edge of maintained travel. Its surviving rootstocks can take the grafts, but the work needs a spell of suitable weather. A timely delivery lets the growers prepare more trees before winter. A damaged or late parcel still has value; fewer grafts take, and the work is harder. The residents have other food and plans. The traveler is contributing to a place, not single-handedly deciding whether everyone survives.

The packing note could read:

> Keep the roots damp and the stems out of standing water. The little grey pear is for the south wall. It came from there before it came to us. If the road turns, bring yourselves home; we can cut another parcel.
>
> — Nessa, Fernwake nursery

Repeated contracts are plausible because an orchard needs many bundles, different varieties, replacement grafts, and equipment. The same scenario can also remain an explicit replay. Replaying a seed does not have to mean that the same historical shipment happened twice.

## A history visible in the landscape

### Paths before wagons

The earliest useful routes followed seasonal movements of wildlife. Brindlehorn grazed along dry contours. Reedskippers used shallow shelves through marshes. Slatewhorls returned to stable gravel beds. People widened selected passages and learned where the animals' requirements differed from a wagon's. A body can squeeze through a gap that an axle cannot.

This history is visible in awkward bends, low branches polished by fur, and old paths continuing beyond the end of a maintained road. A route can be ancient as a passage and almost unknown as a wagon journey.

### The years of small depots

Several generations of travelers established a loose chain of stores and repair yards. Each place maintained the nearest dependable stretch. Markings spread because a traveler needed to recognize a ferry sign before learning the local name for the river.

Roadkeeping is practical, uneven work. A rich orchard valley can replace a bridge promptly; a remote keeper may have to wait for timber and hands. Some old guidebooks describe facilities that no longer exist. There was no single empire that built every road, and there is no lost central authority waiting to be restored.

### The silt year

Within living memory, a prolonged wet season changed Willowford's main channel and buried several low roads. Lantern Reach lost dependable access for two seasons. People moved down-country, taking seeds, tools, and parts of their houses. Others stayed.

The event left specific traces: flood stains above a ferry office door, fruit trees beside empty foundations, an old mileage stone facing a channel that is now grass. Residents disagree about which repairs should come first. The history supplies present choices without requiring a world-ending disaster.

The current expedition season is a renewed attempt to keep the upland connection useful. The road is open, the forecast is limited, and several people would appreciate a fresh account of the country beyond Cairn Gate.

## Life along the road

### Roadkeeping and exchange

Depots share observations through travelers' ledgers. An entry needs a place, a date, and a concrete observation: water over the second notch; three usable boards at the shelter; root patch already dug. Opinions and rumors are marked as such. A well-described failed attempt can help the next traveler.

Coins pay for goods and skilled work. Small favors travel by memory: someone who received a meal may remember your wagon later. Keep favors personal and bounded. There is no universal reputation score that turns kindness into a purchase currency.

Shelters have a common courtesy: leave a dry place for the next arrival if you can. Some travelers do, some cannot, and some take more than they need. A player may spend scarce repair stock on a shelter and receive nothing immediate. Later recognition should be a possible human consequence, not a guaranteed profitable transaction.

Farther from the depots, paper entries give way to marks on stakes and stones. Eventually even those disappear. The journal remains useful because it teaches the player how to observe, rather than supplying an authoritative answer everywhere.

### Material culture

Objects are made to be repaired away from a workshop. Wagon panels are individually replaceable. Rope has flat spliced joins. Food sacks carry a mill mark large enough to read in poor light. A repaired tool may have wood from three places in its handle.

Creature equipment accommodates bodies. Brindlehorn collars leave the antlers free; Reedskipper traces can release before a swim; Cindercoil beds hold warmth without sealing the animal inside. The strongest visual motifs come from this work: graft ties, ferry knots, wheel braces, rain notches, and shelter vents.

Food includes grains, roots, orchard fruit, preserved fish, and other locally available fare. Wildlife feeds on wildlife. Foraging is the current player's direct provisioning activity. A hunting system would require a separate design decision about prey, yield, pursuit, and tone; fighting a hungry Loamling does not produce a meat reward.

### Working with companions

People usually say companions, wildlife, or the species name. Trailomon remains a working game title, not a word everybody must use in conversation.

The animals recognize routines, individuals, places, and learned signals. They do not deliver human dialogue. A lowered head, a repeated wing movement, or a refusal to settle can carry meaning that the player learns. Mechanical limitations must still be written clearly; interpreting an animation must never be the only way to learn that a creature is exhausted.

Travelers teach a few reliable signals during ordinary work. A brace learned beside a broken wheel can become protection during a confrontation. This grounds automatic combat: once an approach is chosen, familiar companions carry out practiced behaviors together. Trust supplies recognition and expressive detail; it does not secretly override a player's plan or become an unlimited power statistic.

## The six companion species

These are ecological and visual extensions of the existing roster. Their proposed combat behaviors are detailed separately; the current build still uses manual commands.

### Brindlehorn

Brindlehorn live on the edges of grassland and open scrub. Their broad antlers have hollow chambers and blunt openings near the tips. Wind moving across the openings produces a low breathy note. Herd members can locate one another beyond a rise before they come into view.

The antler spread helps part flexible brush, while a low center of weight lets the animal lean into resistance. Roadworkers learned to pad the broad inner curves when asking one to brace a load. It must never look like a deer carrying an ornamental rack: the head is low, neck substantial, antlers visibly hollow and useful.

A resting Brindlehorn turns its head to find the wind. A working individual often stops twice before an exposed ridge: once to listen and once to set its feet. Its calm can be mistaken for inexhaustibility. An overworked hauler cannot simply become a fresh defender because battle began.

**Story and play:** a distant antler call suggests open ground beyond a thicket. Moss recognizes an old rubbing post at a shelter. Holding a narrow approach gives a lighter companion room to act, but consumes strength needed to pull tomorrow's wagon.

### Reedskipper

Reedskippers live along margins rather than in deep water. A broad body, webbed feet, and a thick, flattened paddle tail let them work through mud, push off submerged shelves, and turn sharply in a current. Their tail has a clear flat terminal shape that survives a small silhouette.

They drag floating stems into loose resting platforms. Ferrymen noticed that a Reedskipper often chooses a different crossing line from a person looking only at the surface. It feels the bottom and the sideways pull of the water. That skill improves a decision; it cannot make an unsafe crossing harmless.

Individuals enjoy moving objects out of place and watching them float back. Puddle may repeatedly rescue a drifting bowl even when nobody asked. On dry high ground, the same weight that makes the animal stable in water becomes hard work.

**Story and play:** a broken reed platform signals a recent change in flow. A paddle sweep redirects an approaching animal or provides room to withdraw. Preserving Reedskipper's effort for Willowford may be wiser than assigning it every encounter.

### Loamling

Loamlings are compact burrowers with broad digging forelimbs, a sensitive muzzle, and short fur that holds loose soil. Root fibers cling to them after work; they do not need a decorative plant growing from their heads to communicate their habitat.

They uncover bulbs and store food in scattered shallow chambers. Forgotten stores and disturbed soil produce patches of new growth. Experienced travelers recognize the little fans of excavated earth, then check whether an animal still occupies the store before digging nearby.

Buttons presses its muzzle to ground that looks perfectly ordinary and may emerge with something useful or merely interesting. A flat campsite with an energetic Loamling has to be chosen carefully. Wild groups near a wagon can be hungry, displaced, or accustomed to spilled grain; a confrontation has a reason.

**Story and play:** roots mark both food and unstable footing. A defensive hollow can protect a partner when soil allows it. Rocky country reveals the limits of a familiar strategy without making the animal worthless.

### Bellmoth

Bellmoths shelter in dry crevices and fly in the last light. Broad, folded wings resemble a hanging bell when resting. Reflective scales catch weak light in brief shifting bands. They are not miniature glowing fairies, and they do not provide unlimited illumination.

They visit pale flowers and investigate reflective mineral seams. Roadkeepers set small pieces of pale fired clay into important marks; at dusk, a moth's circling can draw attention to a sign almost lost in vegetation. In unmarked country, the same behavior may reveal a mineral face or flowers instead. The player must consider context.

Lumen settles where a lantern does not quite reach. It may open one wing before turning, exposing a narrow flash. Rain mats the wing surface, and a strong crosswind makes precise flying difficult.

**Story and play:** one moth circles a human marker while a whole group follows something farther into the valley. A distracting flash opens space for an ally. Sheltering Bellmoth through bad weather can matter more than taking its help at the first opportunity.

### Cindercoil

Cindercoils live in insulated pockets beneath stones, fallen timber, and old walls. Their rounded bodies curl tightly around a warm center. Food and basking build a store of warmth that can be released over time. After a strong display the animal is cooler, hungry, and tired.

Depots provide vented ceramic resting dishes near drying racks. People offer a safe warm place and a share of food; the animal's presence helps keep damp from settling. A shed's dish can be empty. These relationships do not make every wild individual interested in a wagon.

Ember tests a resting place by uncurling one small section of its body, then folds back in if it is cold. Its expressive movement is opening and closing, with subdued warmth at the seams. It has no permanent flame crown or bright combat aura.

**Story and play:** a nest soaked by runoff is the current recruitment opening. A brief warmth display can discourage a threat, but spending that reserve makes a cold camp harder. The same creature creates a choice between immediate security and tomorrow's comfort.

### Slatewhorl

Slatewhorls live among upland scree and gravel shelves. Their overlapping plates grow in a low spiral around a broad body. The plates resemble local stone in color and wear, but are living growth. Their feet spread when they settle, forming a remarkably stable anchor.

They return to particular sunning places and prefer to reposition slowly. Familiar ground may be worth defending, especially when loose rock leaves few secure places to stand. A blocked path can be an animal's attempt to keep its footing and refuge, rather than a declaration of battle.

Flint turns through several deliberate shuffles before resting. When startled, the outer plates settle in sequence, making a dry ticking sound. Design the whole body around that overlapping turn; avoid a conventional tortoise with random rocks glued onto its shell.

**Story and play:** a pale circular scrape marks a frequently used resting place. A companion can anchor a line or shelter another animal. Repeatedly moving it between defensive positions should be a real weakness, especially on a route that already demands long hauling days.

## The first route

### Fernwake Depot

Fernwake occupies a low, protected bend where a nursery, mill, and wagon yard share water. It is the player's dependable starting point. A worked timber rail carries old collar measurements; the kitchen door is repeatedly repaired at Loamling height.

Nessa tends the nursery and prepares the parcel. She knows plants better than routes and is honest about it. Her practical concern is whether the roots arrived fit to graft, not whether the traveler proved brave.

**Scene:** clipped trees, hanging bundles of graft wood, warm paper labels, overlapping wheel tracks. **Departure hook:** someone asks for an observation beyond the last reliable report. **Return hook:** a remembered companion has its usual place beside the yard.

### Split Oak

Lightning opened the old tree down its center. Both halves survived. The straight meadow route is easy to read in clear weather; the woodland way follows older ground and holds more cover. Travelers attach dated route notes beneath a simple weather hood, sometimes leaving contradictory advice from different seasons.

The oak gives the first lesson in uncertainty: two sensible people can describe the same place differently because they passed it under different conditions.

**Scene:** a split silhouette large enough to recognize from the travel view, one half facing bright grass and one leaning into shade. **Hook:** compare a fresh track with an older written report. Both branches remain viable.

### Mossbell Outpost

The last dependable provision shop has more storage space than living space. Drying shelves, spare rims, and sacks occupy most of the building. Bellmoths gather beneath its deep eaves on calm evenings; a small service bell announces that the keeper is in the yard.

Tovin repairs wheels and trades in supplies. He records the date of every river report because he once sold provisions on the strength of a week-old account and had to help retrieve the stranded wagon. He will extend a conversation, but cannot conjure stock or guarantee the route.

**Scene:** crowded, useful shelter framed against a visibly less maintained road. **Hook:** the last chance to buy something should feel different from the first shop. Beyond this point, the landscape gets more of the screen and reliable advice gets rarer.

### Willowford

The ferry runs beside the remains of an older ford. A measuring post stands near the landing; an older post sits well away from the present channel. Their separation tells the history without an exposition panel.

Sella keeps the ferry and watches how debris moves before judging the current. She trusts observations, including a Reedskipper's, but dislikes confident predictions from people who have not looked at the river. In the prototype the paid ferry remains dependable and available; a lore rumor must not imply a closure the simulation does not support.

**Scene:** water dominates the composition, with the wagon briefly made small. **Hook:** inspect the crossing and learn something that remains useful on another journey. An old roof across the water hints at a former road.

### Cairn Gate

Two stacks of stone frame the final maintained shelter. Beyond them, the ridge is shorter and exposed; the gorge offers cover at the cost of time. The cairns are records of repeated repair, with unlike stones and an imperfect silhouette.

The shelter is usually unstaffed. An occasional surveyor may be present in later content, but arriving here should often mean checking the roof, reading the last entry, and hearing the wind. Several marks indicate places farther out that the current road does not reach.

**Scene:** human construction low in the frame, a large unfamiliar horizon above it. **Hook:** the crew has to supply the reassurance that settlements supplied earlier. The route choice should feel like committing the wagon to country you can see but do not fully know.

### Lantern Reach

Lantern Reach is a small cluster of occupied buildings among older empty plots. During the travel season each occupied house hangs a light facing the approach, allowing arrivals to distinguish the settlement from scattered reflections in the rocks. A light in every window is a meaningful sight because there are so few windows.

Iven maintains the orchard wall and prepares rootstocks with a small seasonal work crew. He has useful knowledge of the immediate slopes and incomplete reports of the valley beyond. On arrival, he asks where the traveler found shelter and how the animals managed the high road.

**Scene:** a few warm points under a large dark landscape, then close views of hands, tools, and the parcel. **Hook:** a successful delivery earns a more ambitious invitation. From the orchard wall, the player can see country no route in the current journal explains.

## Farther expeditions

These are possible later settings, not additions to the first playable. Each removes a different kind of certainty and gives existing companions new relevance.

| Region concept | Landscape and uncertainty | Expedition purpose | Creature and systems direction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The shifting flats | Broad seasonal channels and reed islands; a line on an old map may now be water | Establish a usable passage to an isolated field station | Reedskipper reads shelves; Bellmoth finds old markers; choose observation, ferry materials, or a long dry detour |
| The buried orchard | Fruit trees in dense woodland beyond abandoned terraces; useful food and deceptive openings | Locate surviving varieties and return with a few grafts | Loamling detects cavities beneath turf; Brindlehorn handles brush; harvest quality competes with load and daylight |
| The dry shelves | Wind-cut upland ledges separated by steep loose slopes; no regular resupply | Carry weather instruments and leave a route record | Slatewhorl anchors; Cindercoil supports cold rests; hauling, water access, and retreat distances guide preparation |
| The unmarked valley | A spacious basin reached through a difficult pass; landmarks come from ecology | Reach a defined observation point and return safely | Familiar species behave differently around unfamiliar food and shelter; identify why before committing resources |

Expand geography before adding a large bestiary. A new species should introduce a new way to interpret or traverse a place. Some wildlife should remain wildlife: seeing it need not open a recruitment checklist.

The map should gradually distinguish a measured road, a reported connection, and an unexplored possibility. Reveal useful nearby cues before commitment. Uncertainty can concern distance, abundance, and route connections; it should not conceal the basic cost of a button or turn a rumor into an untelegraphed unavoidable failure.

## Mysteries with practical consequences

The deepest recurring question is why animals maintain some passages long after people stop using them. One abandoned trail still has recently polished bark. Bellmoths repeatedly leave the marked route for the same dark hollow. Slatewhorl resting scrapes trace a line that no survey map joins.

Several explanations can coexist: inherited migration, mineral deposits, seasonal flowers, shelter from a wind humans scarcely notice. Some can be confirmed through observation. Others stay unresolved until a later region reveals the missing context. Avoid making every unusual behavior evidence of the same supernatural force.

The reward for following a mystery should be useful knowledge, a meaningful encounter, a different route, or a specific view. A discovery can also be small and complete: the distant tapping was loose plates on a sleeping Slatewhorl, and the sheltered ledge beside it is dry.

Never withhold essential survival information behind a lore puzzle. A journal entry can suggest a safer approach without becoming a mandatory collectible key.

## Stories that can emerge during play

| Starting circumstance | Choice or observation | Possible later consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moss hauls through two wet days | Rotate the hauler or ask for another hard task | A familiar defender may be tired when scavengers approach |
| A sign points toward an old landing | Compare its date with current river marks | Learn why two route reports disagree |
| A Loamling food store is exposed | Gather nearby, leave it, or take a costly detour to another patch | A later scene can acknowledge disturbed wildlife if that causal flag is actually implemented |
| A shelter has a broken vent | Spend time and repair stock | A future visit may find dry bedding and someone else's repair beside yours |
| A Cindercoil nest is soaked | Offer medicine and dry bedding | The existing follow-up lets the animal seek the wagon |
| Bellmoths leave a familiar sign | Record the direction and conditions | A later expedition can investigate a different seasonal passage |
| A traveler needs food near the last shop | Share enough to matter or preserve reserves | A personal memory or later meeting; no guaranteed refund |
| A wild Slatewhorl occupies a narrow shelf | Wait, redirect it, defend the approach, or detour | The crew pays in time, effort, or condition rather than receiving routine loot |
| The parcel arrives damaged | Tell the recipient what happened | Fewer usable grafts, a specific outcome, and a sensible next contract |
| A party returns before the farthest objective | Preserve a dated observation from the attempt | The next preparation is better informed even though the contract failed |

Except for the existing recruitment and encounter foundations, these are future narrative hooks. Event text may claim a causal connection only when the simulation records it. Random coincidence should not be presented as the player's fault.

## Progression and reasons to return

Three threads can develop across expeditions: the crew's shared history, the extension of usable routes, and the orchard's gradual recovery. Each supports a different kind of attachment.

A returning companion remembers a concrete incident. Later presentation can show Moss hesitating at the same muddy bend or Ember choosing the same dry corner. Keep these recognitions brief and avoid compulsory delays that punish attachment.

Route knowledge opens choices: a different departure point, a specialist contract, or a chance to investigate a reported passage. The first visit can establish a question; a second visit with a different crew can test an answer. Unlocks should broaden preparation rather than make an inexperienced crew numerically obsolete.

The orchard offers visible, modest continuity. A supported future version could show a new graft tied in place, then a few leaves, then a larger work party on a later seasonal visit. Failed deliveries must receive their own honest acknowledgment. Do not display successful growth merely because the player completed any ending.

Persistent world changes require explicit saved flags and a clear chronology. Seed replays should preserve their chosen starting context; ongoing campaign visits can use a newer world state. The current build retains companions, discoveries, memories, and journey records, not an evolving settlement simulation.

## Art sound and language

The world should become visually less organized as travel proceeds. Early scenes have repeated fence posts, level rooflines, and obvious paths. Farther out, plants obscure markers, animal tracks interrupt wheel ruts, and the horizon occupies more space. Preserve readable choices and deliberate composition throughout; wilderness is not an excuse for random visual noise.

Use material motifs with a function: the open center of a hollow antler, a flat paddle tail, overlapping plates, a graft tied to old wood, a ferry post cut with water notches. Echo these forms sparingly in equipment and architecture. Do not cover every object with lore symbols.

The [existing icon language](VISUAL_DIRECTION.md) remains consistent. Grain means provisions, a sprouting cutting means cargo, a hoof print means stamina, and a shield means guard. Future map certainty can use a solid measured line, a broken reported line, and an open unexplored edge, always labeled. Distinctive role icons should be shared between preparation, travel, gathering, and combat.

Sound also marks distance. Fernwake has work and conversation. Mossbell has isolated workshop sounds between longer silences. Willowford masks voices with water. Cairn Gate offers wind and the creak of the wagon. Deeper country introduces recognizable animal calls before their source is seen. Keep a companion's working sound consistent with its combat action.

Dialogue is specific to work, weather, and local experience. People can be funny without constant quips. Tovin might say, "That wheel will get you to the river. Getting you back is what the new rim costs." A field note might say, "The old mark was right about the bank. The bank has moved."

Avoid loading a short event with invented terminology. One unfamiliar name is usually enough. Show a recognizable problem, then let the unusual animal or place supply the wonder.

## A bounded first lore pass

The smallest implementation would revise the contract note and landmark descriptions, add one ecological observation per species, and give each landmark a more specific scenic silhouette. These changes can fit existing screens and the existing eighteen event definitions.

The next pass can bring the two combat encounters into the proposed automatic system, preserving peaceful options and persistent travel costs. Wilderness beyond Lantern Reach, extra contracts, living settlements, new currencies, and a larger roster remain later work.

Before adding a lore detail, identify what it helps the player recognize, choose, anticipate, or care about. Before adding a new system, test whether the same effect can come from the current weather, route, creature, resource, and memory systems.
